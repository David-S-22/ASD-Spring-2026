# Duplicate and recurring charge review

Reference guidance for distinguishing genuine duplicate or runaway recurring
charges from expected, legitimate repeat billing. Repetition alone is not proof
of an anomaly; the pattern around it matters.

## True duplicates
Two identical amounts to the same merchant within a short window — often the
same day — commonly indicate a double-posting or a processing error. The closer
in time and the more exact the amount match, the stronger the signal.

## Expected recurring billing
Subscriptions, utilities, rent, and memberships bill on a regular cadence. A
charge that matches the known amount and schedule of a recognised recurring
payee is expected and should not be flagged just for repeating.

## Price creep on subscriptions
A recurring charge that steps up from its established amount can reflect a price
rise or an unwanted plan change. A single modest increase is weak; a large or
sudden jump on an otherwise steady subscription is worth noting.

## Unexpected cadence changes
A monthly payee that charges weekly, a trial that converts to an unexpectedly
large recurring fee, or a charge that arrives far earlier than its usual date
are cadence anomalies. Compare the interval to the payee's established schedule.

## Partial and incremental repeats
Several smaller charges to the same merchant that together exceed a normal single
purchase can indicate split transactions or card testing. Look at the combined
total and the spacing, not just each charge in isolation.

## Guidance
Only flag repetition when the amount, timing, or cadence breaks the payee's known
pattern. When explaining a duplicate finding, name the matching amount and how
close in time the charges occurred.
